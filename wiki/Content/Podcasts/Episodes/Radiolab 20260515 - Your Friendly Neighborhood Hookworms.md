For most of human history, people went about their daily lives with a worm or two -or fifty- in their guts. Only in the past century, with pharmaceuticals and sanitation practices, have we made significant strides towards deworming the whole of humanity. And that’s typically been thought of as a good thing, because having too many worms in your body can–quite literally -suck the life out of you.

But is it possible to have *too few* worms? Science wonders if deworming ourselves has actually led to an increase in certain chronic diseases. On this episode, we dive into *Necator americanus*, a.k.a. the American Hookworm, and its mysterious relationship with each of us.

## summary (llm)
This *Radiolab* episode explores the complex relationship between humans and **hookworms** (*Necator americanus*). The discussion spans over a century, tracing how these parasites shifted from being viewed as a public health enemy to potential medical tools.

### Historical Context (2:11-10:20)
*   In the early 20th century, *John D. Rockefeller* sought to understand why the American South was economically stagnant. 
*   Commissioners discovered that hookworm infections were causing widespread **anemia** and lethargy among Southerners.
*   The subsequent push for sanitation and the construction of **outhouses** significantly reduced hookworm prevalence, helping the region's economy improve.

### The Hygiene Hypothesis & DIY Treatment (11:45-25:30)
*   The episode highlights the **"hygiene hypothesis,"** which suggests that by eradicating parasites, modern societies may have inadvertently increased the prevalence of autoimmune diseases.
*   Reporter *Pat Walters* profiles *Jasper Lawrence*, a man who suffered from severe allergies and asthma. After reading about the potential health benefits of hookworms, he traveled to Africa to intentionally infect himself, claiming it completely cured his condition.
*   *Lawrence* later attempted to monetize this by shipping hookworms to others, leading to regulatory issues.

### Modern Clinical Research (27:30-44:00)
*   *Molly Webster* provides an update on recent **clinical trials** led by researchers like *Dr. Paul Giacomin*.
*   Studies have explored using hookworms to treat conditions like **Type 2 diabetes**, **celiac disease**, and other autoimmune disorders.
*   The science shows that hookworms survive by secreting proteins that modulate the host's immune system and promote **wound healing**.
*   While participants in trials often report improvements in well-being, the difficulty of standardizing and producing a live-parasite treatment means it remains experimental rather than a mainstream medical therapy.

### Auto-Immune Disease Focus
The video explains that **autoimmune diseases** occur when the body's immune system begins attacking its own tissues rather than just foreign invaders (18:42-19:02). Scientists have identified approximately **80 different types** of these conditions, including **multiple sclerosis**, **Crohn's disease**, and **Type 1 diabetes** (19:01-19:06).

Researchers hypothesize that because hookworms have evolved alongside humans for millions of years, they have developed the ability to **secrete specific proteins** that dampen or "quiet" the immune system (19:42-20:05). This modulation acts as a regulatory check, preventing the immune system from becoming hyperactive and attacking the body, which potentially offers relief for patients suffering from autoimmune disorders (39:50-40:15).
